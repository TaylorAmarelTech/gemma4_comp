You are not in trouble, and what is happening to you is not your fault. Based on what you describe — your passport taken, a large recruitment fee you had to pay, and wages now being withheld at the factory — you are very likely a victim of forced labour and human trafficking as those terms are defined in international law. That matters, because it means the law is built to protect *you* and to hold the recruiter and employer responsible, not the other way around. Please read this carefully; I will explain what is happening, exactly what you are entitled to, and how to seek help **safely**.

---

**First, your safety and a few records**

- If you are in immediate physical danger, getting to a safe place comes before anything else here.
- When it is safe to do so, quietly keep or photograph anything that proves your situation: your contract or job offer, any receipt or transfer showing the recruitment fee you paid, records of the hours you worked and the wages you were or were not paid, the agency's and employer's names, and — if you can see it — a photo of your own passport and its details. Be careful if your phone or messages might be monitored by the employer.
- You have a right to your passport. It is your property. Under the **International Convention on the Protection of the Rights of All Migrant Workers (ICRMW, 1990), Article 21**, it is unlawful for anyone other than an authorised public official to confiscate or hold your identity or travel documents. The employer keeping it is itself a violation.

---

**Why international law is on your side (the reasoning that defines your case)**

Human trafficking under the **UN Palermo Protocol (2000), Article 3(a)** is made of three parts — an **Act**, a **Means**, and a **Purpose**. Your situation appears to meet all three:

- **Act** — you were *recruited* and are now *harboured* at the factory.
- **Means** — you describe *deception* about the job and its costs, *abuse of your position of vulnerability* as a migrant in debt, *debt bondage* through the large fee, and *retention of your passport* and *withholding of wages* as coercion to keep you working.
- **Purpose** — *exploitation* in the form of *forced labour*, defined by **ILO Convention No. 29 (1930), Article 2(1)** as work exacted under the menace of a penalty that you did not truly offer yourself for voluntarily. Here the "penalty" is real: you cannot leave without your passport, you lose the fee you already paid, and you forfeit the wages you are owed.

Three of the **ILO's recognised indicators of forced labour** are present in your message at once — *retention of identity documents*, *debt bondage*, and *withholding of wages*. And under **Palermo Article 3(b)**, the fact that you may have initially "agreed" to the job or signed something is legally irrelevant once these coercive means are used. The recruitment fee itself is prohibited: **ILO Convention No. 181 (1997), Article 7** says agencies must not charge workers fees, directly or indirectly, and the **ILO Employer Pays Principle** (ILO General Principles and Operational Guidelines for Fair Recruitment, 2016/2019) puts the cost of recruitment on the employer, never the worker. **ILO Convention No. 105 (1957)** and the migrant-worker conventions **No. 97 (1949)** and **No. 143 (1975)** reinforce that none of this is lawful.

---

**You cannot be punished for what was done to you**

This is important so that fear does not stop you from seeking help. The **non-punishment principle** means you should not be prosecuted or penalised — including for immigration status, overstaying, or anything you were forced to do — because those things are a direct consequence of being trafficked. This is set out in the **2014 Protocol to ILO Convention No. 29 (P029), Article 4(2)**, and in the **OHCHR Recommended Principles and Guidelines on Human Rights and Human Trafficking (2002), Principle 7** (and, in Europe, **Article 26 of the Council of Europe "Warsaw" Convention, 2005**). If your visa or status became irregular because the employer held your documents, that is part of the abuse — not a crime by you.

---

**The full set of remedies you may be entitled to**

You do not have to choose only one of these; several can run at the same time, and a good advocate will help you pursue all that apply in your country.

1. **Recovery of your unpaid wages.** Withheld wages are recoverable through the labour authority, labour inspectorate, or labour court/tribunal, usually including back pay and often overtime and penalties. Equal treatment in pay is protected by **ICRMW Article 25**.
2. **Refund of your recruitment fee (Employer Pays).** Because the fee was unlawful under **ILO C181 Art. 7** and the **Employer Pays Principle**, you can claim full reimbursement. In the **electronics sector specifically**, if the factory supplies international brands, those brands' supplier codes and the **Responsible Business Alliance (RBA) Code of Conduct** already require zero worker-paid fees and full repayment — this is a strong, practical pressure point an advocate can use.
3. **Compensation for harm suffered**, beyond wages and fees — for the exploitation itself. This right is in **Palermo Protocol Article 6(6)** and **ILO P029 Article 4(1)**, and can come through criminal restitution orders, civil claims, or state victim-compensation funds.
4. **Criminal complaint** against the recruiter and employer for trafficking and forced labour (the passport-taking, debt bondage, and wage theft are evidence of the offence). The state, not you, carries the prosecution.
5. **Civil claim** for your unpaid wages, the fee, and damages (including for unjust enrichment from the fee).
6. **Labour inspection / labour tribunal.** A labour inspectorate can intervene at the workplace, and a labour tribunal can order payment without you needing to prove a criminal case.
7. **Visa and immigration protection.** As a presumed trafficking victim you may be entitled to a temporary residence or reflection period **decoupled from this employer**, so your status no longer depends on the people exploiting you (see **Palermo Article 7**; in Europe, **Warsaw Convention Articles 13–14**; many countries have an equivalent victim visa). Do not let fear about your visa stop you from seeking help — securing protected status is itself one of the remedies.
8. **Free legal aid and information.** You have the right to information about proceedings and to legal assistance to present your case (**Palermo Article 6(2)**); trafficking victims can often access legal aid at no cost.
9. **Support services** — safe shelter, medical care, and counselling during a recovery period (**Palermo Article 6(3)**; **Warsaw Convention Articles 12–13**).
10. **Safe, voluntary repatriation if and when you choose it.** Return home should be safe and preferably voluntary, with a risk assessment so you are not re-trafficked (**Palermo Article 8**). The **International Organization for Migration (IOM)** assists with voluntary return and reintegration. Importantly, **leaving the country does not erase your wage, fee, and compensation claims** — those can often still be pursued.
11. **A complaint to the recruitment regulator** in both the country you work in and your home country, against the licensed agency that charged the fee — this can trigger fines, licence suspension, and reimbursement, and protects the next worker after you.
12. **Your embassy or consulate.** Your own country's mission can be one of your safest first contacts: it can issue an emergency travel document if your passport is not returned, refer you to vetted lawyers and shelters, and raise your case with local authorities.

---

**Who to approach — and who to avoid (this part protects you)**

Not every "official" door is a safe one, so choose carefully:

- **Do not** go back to the recruitment agency, and do not rely on anyone connected to your employer to "sort it out." They have an interest in keeping you silent.
- **Be cautious with local police as a first step.** In some places they may treat you as an immigration offender, or may be connected to the people exploiting you. That does not mean never involve the authorities — it means approach them *through* a trusted intermediary who can confirm it is safe and go with you.
- **Safer first contacts** are usually: **your own country's embassy or consulate**; a reputable **migrant-worker NGO** or **trade union**; the **national anti-trafficking hotline or referral mechanism**; or international bodies like the **ILO** and **IOM**. These can assess which local institutions are trustworthy, accompany you, and make sure your protections (non-punishment, victim visa, legal aid) are asserted from the start.
- I am deliberately not giving you a phone number from memory, because the right, current contact depends on exactly where you are, and a wrong number could send you to the wrong door. Ask one trusted source above for the verified hotline and a vetted lawyer in your specific country and city, and let them coordinate the rest.

---

**A safe order to take this in**

1. Secure your personal safety and quietly preserve your records.
2. Make **one** safe first contact — your embassy/consulate, a trusted migrant-worker NGO or union, or the national anti-trafficking hotline.
3. Through them, get a **vetted lawyer / legal aid** and have your **trafficking-victim status and non-punishment protection** asserted.
4. With that support in place, pursue the wage claim, the fee refund, compensation, the criminal and civil cases, the labour-tribunal claim, the regulator complaint, and your visa protection — and decide on safe repatriation only when you are ready.

You have done nothing wrong by being deceived, indebted, and exploited. International law treats you as a person owed protection and remedy — your wages, your fee back, compensation, your documents, your freedom to leave, and your safety. The most important next step is the smallest one: reach **one** safe contact, and let them help you take the rest one step at a time.
